# Research and writing rules

## Research

- **Look for the evidence against the idea first.** The closest prior art, failed attempts, and the
  strongest counter-evidence tell you more than supporting material, and they're what a critical
  reader will check.
- **Prefer primary sources:** papers, laws and regulations, official statistics, company filings,
  first-hand reports. Use secondary coverage to find primary sources, not to replace them.
- **Date everything about the present.** "As of 2026-09", "the 2025 annual report". Current-state
  claims go stale; a date lets a reader judge them.
- **Never invent a citation.** Only cite what you actually opened. If a claim matters but you can't
  verify it, mark it `[unverified]` or drop it. A fabricated reference destroys a paper's
  credibility, and a skeptical reviewer will check.
- **Name the prior art.** Knowing who got there first is how you find what's genuinely new; say what
  this idea adds.
- **Numbered references** at the end, with links, cited in the text as [1], [2].
- **Keep a research trail.** As you go, note every source you open. Cited sources go in References;
  the rest go in an `### Also consulted` list under it, with links, including sources that argued
  against the idea and were answered or set aside. The reader should be able to see everything that
  informed the paper, not only what made the final cut, so that any claim can be traced later.

## Style

- Write prose that argues. Don't chain one-sentence paragraphs, and use bullet lists only for real
  lists.
- Hedge where the uncertainty actually is, and say what it is. "Potentially" in every sentence hedges
  nothing.
- Define terms before relying on them, and keep using the same word for the same thing. If the
  argument depends on "better", say better by what measure.
- Prefer causal chains ("A lowers the cost of B, which lets C …") to lists of possibilities. Where
  the argument forks, say what decides which branch we end up on.
- Rough orders of magnitude beat adjectives.
- Bold at most one phrase per section.
- The Summary must stand on its own: someone who reads only it should know the claim, the mechanism,
  the biggest uncertainty, and what would change the author's mind.

## Sections by kind

The template lists optional sections for each kind; insert them after "The argument".

- **venture:** name a specific customer and what they use and pay for today. Unit economics can be a
  sketch, but it must exist. "Go-to-market" means the first ten customers, not a funnel diagram.
  "Cheapest test of demand" is the most important next step.
- **product:** start from users and their problem; explain why existing solutions fall short before
  describing yours.
- **research:** hypotheses stated so they can fail, the method, the data needed, and what each
  possible result would mean, including the boring one.
- **policy:** compare options (including doing nothing), map stakeholders and their incentives, and
  say how it would be enforced.
- **project:** who it serves and how they're involved in shaping it, how it sustains itself after
  launch, and who could be harmed.
